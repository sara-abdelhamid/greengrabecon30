GreenGrab

Research Question

When a corporation buys a carbon credit from an African forest, who actually pays the price?

The voluntary carbon market is one of the fastest-growing corners of climate finance. In large parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, it is also displacing the same communities that colonialism displaced a century ago. The paperwork is different now. The logic is not.

By Sara  ·  ECON 30: Progress and Inequality  ·  Spring 2026 Scroll

Part I

What Is a Carbon Credit, and What Does It Actually Buy?

A carbon credit is a certificate representing one metric tonne of CO2 prevented from entering the atmosphere. A company with a net-zero pledge buys these credits to offset its own emissions. The market is voluntary, meaning no government requires it. The prices are set by private certification bodies, mainly Verra and Gold Standard. And a growing share of the most traded credits come from forests in Africa.

The mechanism sounds clean. A forest that would have been cleared is instead preserved, a credit is issued, and a corporation somewhere claims it has balanced its books on carbon. What this story leaves out is that the forests being enrolled in these projects are not empty. They are farmed, grazed, and governed by communities whose land rights are often customary rather than formally registered. Those rights are invisible to the registries that issue the credits.

That last step is the argument. Credits worth millions on global markets are generated from land whose communities receive almost nothing in return, and often lose access to the forests they have managed for generations. A 2023 Oakland Institute report on Uganda found communities evicted with no compensation and no legal recourse. Their land had become an accounting entry for someone else's emissions.

The mechanism is different, but the structure is not: distant powers are financializing African land for their own benefit, while local communities absorb the cost.

Part II

The Land: What Has Actually Happened

The carbon land grab is not abstract. It has specific geography, specific corporate actors, and specific communities. Three cases show the pattern clearly.

Zimbabwe: Kariba REDD+

Kariba was one of the largest carbon credit projects in the world, covering 785,000 hectares in Zimbabwe. Shell, Volkswagen, and Delta Air Lines bought credits from it as part of their net-zero strategies. A 2023 Guardian investigation using independent analysis by Carbon Plan found that up to 94% of Kariba's credits may have been phantom, generated against a deforestation baseline that was never realistic. The communities in the buffer zone lost access to forest resources they had depended on for subsistence, and revenue sharing promises in the project documents went largely unfulfilled.

Liberia: Blue Carbon and the UAE Deal

In 2022, the government of Liberia signed a deal with Blue Carbon LLC, a company registered in Dubai, giving it rights over roughly 10% of Liberia's total land area. The deal was signed without public disclosure. Reporting by Vice and the Financial Times found that contract terms gave Blue Carbon broad authority to restrict community land use, with revenue sharing heavily weighted toward the company. A coalition of more than 40 civil society organizations called it a new scramble for Africa's forests and demanded cancellation.

Kenya: Kajiado and the Maasai

In Kajiado County, Maasai communities have faced carbon projects that restrict seasonal grazing on land their families have used for generations. A 2024 Greenpeace Africa investigation found that community consent was obtained through meetings that excluded women and young herders, a majority of the affected population. Contracts were signed by male elders who, under Maasai customary law, do not have unilateral authority to give up grazing rights for the whole community. Legal challenges have since been filed.

Part III

The Pattern Is Not New

These cases are not isolated failures in an otherwise fair system. They are what the system produces when it is built on top of land tenure frameworks inherited from colonial dispossession.

Colonial land alienation in Africa rested on a set of interlocking assumptions: that African land tenure was informal and therefore not real property; that communities were failing to use the land "productively" by the colonizer's standards; and that external actors had both the right and the obligation to put that land to better use. Under these assumptions, British, French, Belgian, and Portuguese administrations transferred vast tracts of land to European settlers and companies. Communities whose livelihoods depended on this land had no standing in the courts that governed the transfers.

Consent procedure None required; colonial law recognized no such right FPIC required on paper; routinely bypassed in practice
Who certifies the taking Colonial administration Verra, Gold Standard, and third-party auditors paid by project developers

Scholars at Makerere, SOAS, and in the Review of African Political Economy have traced this lineage directly. The land tenure systems that make communities vulnerable to carbon grabs today are, in many cases, the exact systems installed by colonial administrations to enable land alienation then. A 2011 analysis (SAFLII) documented how customary tenure, stripped of formal recognition under colonial law, remains legally precarious across dozens of post-independence African states. That precarity is what carbon project developers exploit now.

This does not mean carbon markets cannot work. It means that a carbon market built on institutions inherited from colonial dispossession will reproduce that dispossession unless it is deliberately restructured.

"Green colonialism" is not a metaphor. It describes how the legal infrastructure of colonial land alienation is being reactivated, with carbon registries playing the role that colonial land courts once did.

Part IV

Progress for Whom?

The central question of this course is about progress and inequality: who gains from growth, who gets left behind, and what determines the answer. The voluntary carbon market is, on one reading, a genuine institutional innovation. It prices an externality that had been priced at zero. That matters. But "progress" is distributed very unevenly across this story.

Who gains

Corporations can continue emitting while claiming neutrality. Carbon project developers capture the bulk of credit revenue. The global accounting system gets offsets that allow the fiction of net-zero without structural decarbonization. Wealthy-country governments gain flexibility in their Paris commitments.

The bottleneck

The core bottleneck is property rights. Carbon markets require legal clarity about who owns sequestration rights. Without strong community tenure, that clarity defaults to whoever holds formal title, usually the state, a developer, or an investor. Communities with robust customary rights but no formal title are excluded from the gains and exposed to the losses.

This inverts the logic de Soto describes. The carbon market has formalized the asset (sequestration rights) for investors while leaving the underlying land rights of communities informal and therefore unprotected. The result is a transfer, not a market exchange.

What this means for the future

The Africa Carbon Markets Initiative (ACMI), launched at COP27, wants Africa to become the dominant carbon credit supplier globally. If current institutional conditions persist, scaling ACMI means scaling dispossession. The question is not whether Africa should participate in carbon markets. It is whether those markets can be redesigned so that participation means wealth creation rather than extraction. That requires mandatory community land titling before certification, genuine revenue-sharing floors, and certifiers that are independent of the developers who pay their fees.

The Tanzania Kilosa data makes the stakes concrete. Households in REDD+-enrolled villages were measurably poorer and less food-secure than comparable households in non-enrolled villages, even after receiving carbon payments. The payments averaged under $10 per household per year. The growth path those families gave up was worth far more. The carbon market, in that case, re-routed their development through a financial instrument that captured the value for someone else and sent the costs home.

Conclusion

The Takeaway

When a corporation buys a carbon credit from an African forest, the price it pays flows mostly to a project developer, a certification body, and a government. Almost none of it reaches the community whose land and labor is the foundation of the whole transaction. That community, meanwhile, loses access to the forest it has managed for generations. This is not a bug. Given the land tenure institutions the market operates within, it is the expected outcome.

The voluntary carbon market is not necessarily a false solution to climate change. But in its current form, it is a mechanism for converting African communities' most valuable asset, their relationship to the land, into a financial instrument that pays everyone except them. Calling this climate action while ignoring the dispossession it produces is using the language of progress to do the work of extraction.

Reforming this requires more than tighter registry standards. It requires a prior commitment: no carbon right can be created from land where the underlying human rights, the rights of communities to their land and livelihoods, have not been formally recognized and protected. Without that commitment, the scramble for African land in the name of carbon will continue.

One takeaway line: A carbon credit is a certificate of emissions reduction for the buyer, and a certificate of land alienation for the community.
