The Carbon Credit Was Supposed to Save the Planet.

Research Question

When a corporation buys a carbon credit from an African forest, who actually pays the price?

Carbon credits were designed as a mechanism for progress: market-driven climate action that would simultaneously fund development in the Global South. Two decades later, the investigations tell a different story. The credits were often fictitious. The forests were often taken. And the communities who lived in them are still paying.

By Sara

Part I

Carbon Credits Were Designed as a Progress Mechanism

In 1997, the Kyoto Protocol introduced a radical idea: let markets find the cheapest way to reduce global emissions. If cutting carbon is cheaper in Brazil than in Germany, Germany should pay for it there. This became the Clean Development Mechanism, the first international carbon credit system, and the template for everything that followed.

The theory was genuinely optimistic. Rich countries would fund emissions reductions in poor countries at a fraction of the domestic cost. The host countries would receive investment and technology. The planet would benefit from lower emissions overall. Climate action and development, delivered together by the invisible hand.

The Voluntary Carbon Market, or VCM, emerged in the 2000s as the unregulated private version of the same idea. No government required companies to buy these credits. They did it to meet their own net-zero pledges, driven by shareholder pressure, ESG ratings, and the growing expectation from consumers that corporations take responsibility for their emissions. By 2021, the VCM was valued at over $2 billion annually, with projections reaching $50 billion by 2030.

Africa became the primary target. The continent holds roughly 25% of the world's tropical forests and some of the largest intact grassland and savanna ecosystems on earth. It also has weaker land titling systems, lower enforcement capacity, and communities whose land rights are often customary rather than formally registered. In the logic of carbon markets, this translated to abundant, cheap supply. By 2023, Africa was issuing an estimated 165 million voluntary carbon credits annually, more than any other region.

That last step is the argument. Credits worth millions on global markets are generated from land whose communities receive almost nothing in return, and often lose access to the forests they have managed for generations. This is not a failure of individual projects. It is a structural outcome of building a financial market on top of land tenure systems designed — in many cases, during colonial administration — to make community rights legally invisible.

Verra (which issues Verified Carbon Units) and Gold Standard are the two dominant certification bodies. Together they have issued billions of credits. They are private, non-governmental organizations funded primarily by fees from the project developers whose credits they certify.

This is a structural conflict of interest that has parallels in another financial scandal. Rating agencies in the 2008 financial crisis were paid by the banks whose mortgage-backed securities they rated. The incentive was to approve, not to scrutinize. Carbon certification operates on the same model: the certifier is paid by the party seeking certification. Independent auditors are also paid by the developer. There is no regulator with binding authority to reject a project's baseline assumptions.

Verra's response to the Kariba revelations was to acknowledge that its methodologies for setting baselines needed review. The baselines — the counterfactual "what would have happened without the project" — are the mechanism through which phantom credits are generated. If you claim the forest would have been 400% more deforested than comparable forests actually are, you can generate four times as many credits as legitimate. Verra's methodology did not prevent this. It arguably enabled it.

The mechanism is different, but the structure is not: distant powers are again financializing African land for their own benefit, while the communities on that land absorb the cost.
Sara, synthesis of Oakland Institute (2023), ROAPE (2024), and Fern (2023)

Part II

What the Investigations Found

In January 2023, The Guardian and the nonprofit research group Carbon Plan published the results of a year-long investigation into Kariba REDD+, at the time one of the world's largest carbon offset projects. What they found upended the voluntary carbon market's claims about itself.

Kariba covered 785,000 hectares of miombo woodland in Zimbabwe. For nearly two decades, it issued carbon credits to some of the world's largest corporations: Shell, Volkswagen, Delta Air Lines, and dozens of others bought Kariba credits as part of their net-zero strategies. The credits were Verra-certified. Third-party auditors had reviewed them repeatedly. The project was, by any formal measure, legitimate.

Carbon Plan's analysis found that Kariba had set its counterfactual deforestation baseline at roughly four times the actual deforestation rate in comparable forests. This meant the project could claim credit for preventing deforestation that was never realistically going to happen. Up to 94% of its 2.4 million issued credits may have represented emissions reductions that did not exist. The corporations that bought them were, in the most precise sense, paying for nothing.

Kariba was not isolated. An Earthsight investigation the following year found phantom credits across multiple African projects, each using similar baseline manipulation. The systematic nature of the problem pointed not to rogue developers but to a certification methodology that created incentives for inflation and lacked the independent oversight to catch it.

The Liberia Deal

In late 2022, the government of Liberia signed agreements with Blue Carbon LLC, a company registered in Dubai, covering approximately one million hectares of Liberian forest — about 10% of the country's total land area. The contracts were signed without public disclosure, without parliamentary approval, and without consultation with the communities living in and around the affected land. Reporting by Vice, the Financial Times, and CorpWatch found that contract terms gave Blue Carbon sweeping authority over land use, logging rights, and carbon revenue, with minimal accountability to the Liberian state or its citizens.

Over forty African civil society organizations issued a joint statement calling it "a new scramble for Africa's forests." The legal mechanism was a government-to-company agreement that bypassed community tenure entirely. The communities in the project area had no legal standing to object because their customary rights over the forest had no formal recognition in Liberian law — a direct legacy of colonial land legislation that converted customary lands to "public lands" under state control.

To issue a carbon credit, a project must demonstrate that it has prevented emissions that "would have occurred" without the project. This requires establishing a baseline: a model of what the forest would look like if the project did not exist. The baseline is not a measurement. It is a prediction about a counterfactual world.

Verra's methodology allows project developers to draw their baseline from a "reference region" — nearby forests that are supposedly similar to the project area. If those reference forests have high deforestation rates, the project can claim large emissions reductions even if the project forest would not have been meaningfully deforested. For Kariba, Carbon Plan found that the reference regions included forests with deforestation rates far higher than the project area had historically experienced. The result was a baseline that overstated the emissions threat by roughly 400%.

The perverse incentive is structural. The more threatened you can make your forest appear, the more credits you can sell. The developer wants a high baseline. The certifier is paid by the developer. The auditor is paid by the developer. The community has no seat at the table. There is no one in this chain whose financial interest is served by an accurate, conservative baseline.

In Uganda, the Oakland Institute documented communities in Kiboga and Mubende evicted from land they had farmed for decades to make way for New Forests Company plantations generating carbon and timber credits. Compensation was negligible. Legal processes were absent. Families who returned to their land reported being threatened and having crops destroyed.

In Madagascar, a New Lines Magazine investigation (2024) found that communities relying on forest-edge agriculture inside Makira Natural Park faced intensified enforcement since the carbon project began. Park rangers, partially funded by credit revenues, had destroyed crops and confiscated land. Revenues from $18 million in total credit sales flowed primarily to the Wildlife Conservation Society and the Malagasy government. Affected households received less than 3%.

In Tanzania's Kilosa District, a peer-reviewed 2023 study in World Development compared enrolled and non-enrolled villages. Enrolled households showed a statistically significant 14% decline in caloric sufficiency compared to control villages, even after receiving carbon payments. The payments averaged under $10 per household per year, far below the value of the agricultural expansion they were prevented from pursuing.

This last finding matters as an economic argument, not just a moral one. The enrolled communities were measurably worse off in material terms. The carbon market, in that case, did not deliver development alongside conservation. It substituted a tiny cash transfer for the foregone growth path of smallholder farming, and the substitution was deeply unfavorable for the communities.

Part IV

This Land Has Been Taken Before

The cases above are not the product of bad actors exploiting an otherwise sound system. They are what the system reliably produces when it operates on top of a specific legal infrastructure: one inherited from the colonial reorganization of African land tenure.

Colonial land alienation rested on interlocking assumptions. African tenure systems were informal and therefore not "real" property under European law. African communities were failing to use land "productively" by colonial standards. External actors had both the right and the duty to improve that land's use. Under these premises, colonial administrations transferred vast tracts across the continent to European settlers and companies. The communities whose livelihoods depended on that land had no standing in the courts governing the transfers.

The specific legal mechanism was the doctrine of "waste lands" and "crown lands," through which uncultivated or communally managed land was classified as belonging to the state. Post-independence African governments largely inherited these classifications. A government-to-company agreement like the Liberia Blue Carbon deal does not require the government to consult the communities using the forest, because the forest is legally the government's to lease. That legal reality traces directly to colonial land legislation.

Scholars at Makerere, SOAS, and in the Review of African Political Economy have traced this lineage precisely. The land tenure precarity that makes communities vulnerable to carbon grabs is not a development failure. It is a colonial design feature that was never dismantled.

Verra and Gold Standard both require Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC) from communities before a project can be certified. FPIC is a principle derived from UN declarations on Indigenous peoples' rights and is, on paper, a meaningful standard. In practice, carbon project FPIC processes have been documented as deeply inadequate across multiple African projects.

In Kajiado County, Kenya, a 2024 Greenpeace Africa investigation found consent obtained through meetings that excluded women and young herders — the people most dependent on restricted grazing land. Contracts were signed by male elders whose authority under Maasai customary law does not extend to unilaterally alienating grazing rights from the full community. Yet the project was certified as community-consented.

The structural issue is that FPIC audits are conducted by auditors paid by developers. There is no independent verification of whether consent was meaningful. Communities that object after the fact have no legal mechanism to challenge a Verra certification. The consent process exists on paper; the legal architecture for enforcing it does not.

"Green colonialism" is not a metaphor. It describes how the legal infrastructure of colonial land alienation is being reactivated, with carbon registries playing the role that colonial land courts once did.
Sara, synthesis of Olanya (ECAS4), Laltaika and Askew (UN, 2018), Fern REDD+ Briefing (2023)

Part VI

Progress for Some: The Political Economy of Green Finance

Carbon credits were framed as a win-win: climate progress and economic development, delivered simultaneously. The data suggests a more precise outcome. They delivered climate progress — of debatable quality — for the buyers, and economic regression for many of the communities whose land made those credits possible.

The Tanzania Kilosa study is the clearest evidence. It uses a quasi-experimental design — enrolled and non-enrolled villages in the same district, comparable on observable characteristics — and finds that enrollment in REDD+ was associated with a measurable decline in household welfare. The carbon payments did not compensate for the foregone agricultural growth. This is not an anecdote. It is causal evidence that a climate mechanism designed to produce development co-benefits produced the opposite.

Who gains

Corporations purchase the right to continue emitting while claiming neutrality. Project developers capture 55–65% of credit revenue. Global carbon accounting gains offsets that delay the structural decarbonization of high-emitting industries. Wealthy-country governments gain flexibility in Paris Agreement commitments.

The real bottleneck: property rights

Carbon markets require legal clarity about who controls sequestration rights. Without formally recognized community tenure, that clarity defaults to whoever holds state title, typically the government or a developer. This is precisely the bottleneck that development economists have identified as a barrier to wealth creation in the Global South — the inability of informal assets to function as legal capital.

Carbon markets have inverted this logic. They formalized one layer of the asset — sequestration rights — for investors, while leaving the underlying land rights of communities informal and therefore vulnerable. The market created capital from the forest without creating rights for the people in it. The result is not a market. It is a transfer.

The Africa Carbon Markets Initiative, launched at COP27 in Egypt in 2022, was presented as African ownership of carbon finance. It was endorsed by African finance ministers and positioned as a mechanism for African governments to direct climate revenue toward domestic development. The initiative's stated goal is to make Africa the world's leading carbon credit supplier, targeting 300 million credits annually by 2030.

The problem is institutional. ACMI operates within the same VCM architecture that has produced the outcomes documented above. African governments may receive higher revenue shares. But revenue flowing to governments does not flow to communities. The Liberia Blue Carbon deal was signed by the Liberian government. That is a government-level transaction. The communities in the project area remained, legally and practically, excluded.

There is a version of African carbon market sovereignty that would genuinely benefit communities: mandatory land titling before project certification, community-controlled carbon rights, and benefit-sharing floors enforced by African regulatory bodies rather than developer-funded auditors. ACMI, in its current form, is not that. It is a scaling of the existing model with African government endorsement attached.

Conclusion

The Verdict

Carbon credits were created as an answer to an inequality: the externalization of climate costs by rich countries onto everyone. They were supposed to correct a market failure. What they built in Sub-Saharan Africa, on the foundation of inherited colonial law, is a new externality. The communities whose forests generate the credits bear the costs of conservation. The corporations whose emissions make those forests economically valuable capture the benefits. The mechanism is a market in name and a transfer in effect.

This does not mean the idea of carbon markets is irredeemable. It means that a carbon market built on unrecognized land rights will reproduce dispossession. It means the certification architecture, funded by developers and audited by auditors hired by developers, cannot catch the fraud it has structural incentives to overlook. And it means that ACMI's ambition to make Africa the world's largest carbon supplier, if pursued within this framework, is an ambition to scale the problem.

What a legitimate alternative looks like is not complicated, even if it is politically difficult: mandatory community land titling before any carbon project can be certified; revenue-sharing minimums enforced by statute, not by developer choice; certification bodies funded publicly or by buyers, not developers; and meaningful legal standing for communities to challenge registrations that cover their land without their real consent.

The carbon credit, as it currently operates in much of Africa, is a certificate of emissions reduction for the buyer and a certificate of land alienation for the community. Progress on climate. Regression on everything else. That is not a win-win. It is the same arithmetic that governed the colonial resource economy: the gains go to whoever controls the legal architecture, and the costs go to whoever does not.
