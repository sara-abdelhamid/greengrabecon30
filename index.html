Who Pays When Corporations Buy Carbon Credits from African Forests?

Research Question

When a corporation buys a carbon credit from an African forest, who actually pays the price?

By Sara

The tension at the heart of climate finance is this: the countries that caused the problem have the resources to solve it, and the countries bearing the worst impacts do not. Carbon credits were designed as the bridge—a market mechanism that would channel capital from wealthy emitters to climate-vulnerable regions, funding both emissions reductions and local development. Two decades in, that bridge looks more like extraction.

Today, the voluntary carbon market is in crisis. Major investigations have found that up to 94% of credits from the world's largest forest offset projects may represent emissions reductions that never happened (West et al., 2023; Badgley et al., 2024). Corporations paid hundreds of millions for climate neutrality claims built on inflated baselines and phantom conservation. But the accounting fraud is only half the story.

The other half is happening on the ground in Sub-Saharan Africa, which now supplies more forest carbon credits than any other region. Communities are being dispossessed of land they've managed for generations—not through outright seizure, but through contracts signed by governments, certified by auditors paid by developers, and justified by the language of climate action. The legal mechanism is familiar: customary land rights that aren't formally recognized become "available" for external actors to financialize. The justification has changed from civilization and development to sustainability and conservation. The structure hasn't.

Scholars and civil society organizations across Africa have termed this "green colonialism"—not as metaphor, but as description. The same legal infrastructure that enabled colonial land alienation is being reactivated: state control over lands classified as "public" or "unimproved," disregard for customary tenure systems, external certification of contracts that bypass community consent, and financial flows that extract value while externalizing costs onto local populations (Olanya, ECAS4; Laltaika & Askew, 2018; ROAPE, 2024). Carbon registries are playing the role that colonial land courts once did.

Part I

The Promise — Markets Would Save the Planet and Fund Development

Carbon credits are financial instruments designed to put a price on emissions reductions. One credit represents one metric ton of CO₂ either prevented from entering the atmosphere or removed from it. The basic theory: if a company in Germany can't cheaply reduce its own emissions, it can pay for emissions reductions somewhere else—like protecting a forest in Zimbabwe that would otherwise be cleared—and count those reductions against its own climate targets.

This mechanism was formalized in 1997 under the Kyoto Protocol's Clean Development Mechanism (CDM), the first international carbon credit system. The idea was simple: rich countries fund emissions cuts in poor countries at a fraction of domestic cost. Host countries receive investment and technology transfer. The planet gets lower emissions overall. Climate action and development, delivered together.

The Voluntary Carbon Market (VCM) emerged in the 2000s as the private, unregulated version of this system. No government mandated participation—companies bought credits voluntarily to meet their own net-zero pledges, driven by shareholder pressure, ESG ratings, and consumer expectations. By 2021, the VCM was worth over $2 billion annually, with projections hitting $50 billion by 2030 (Ecosystem Marketplace, 2021).

Within this market, REDD+ (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation) became the dominant project type. A developer establishes a protected area, gets it certified by Verra or Gold Standard, and sells credits to corporations who can then claim carbon neutrality. These aren't renewable energy projects—they're payments to not cut down forests.

Africa became the primary supplier. The continent holds approximately 25% of the world's tropical forests (FAO, 2020), vast savanna ecosystems, and—critically for developers—weaker formal land tenure systems. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization found that less than 10% of rural land in Sub-Saharan Africa is formally documented, with customary systems governing most productive land but lacking legal recognition (FAO, Voluntary Guidelines on Land Tenure, 2020). In carbon market terms, this made African projects cheaper and faster to establish. By 2023, Africa was issuing 165 million voluntary carbon credits annually—more than any other region (Ecosystem Marketplace, 2023). Investigations found that African projects showed higher rates of community displacement and lower revenue-sharing ratios than comparable projects in Latin America, attributed primarily to differences in land tenure documentation (Forest Peoples Programme, 2023).

How carbon credits are generated and sold

Step 1: Developer identifies a forested area and negotiates rights to the carbon stored in it—through direct purchase, long-term lease, or government agreement.

Step 2: Developer establishes a "baseline"—a projection of how much deforestation would occur without the project. This becomes the counterfactual against which avoided emissions are measured.

Step 3: Third-party auditors verify the baseline and project design according to standards set by Verra or Gold Standard. The auditors are paid by the developer.

Step 4: Credits are issued—one per metric ton of CO₂ claimed as avoided emissions. Registered and sold on voluntary markets.

Step 5: Corporations purchase credits to offset emissions, claiming carbon neutrality in sustainability reports.

Step 6: Revenue flows primarily to the developer and certification intermediaries. Communities receive a small fraction—if anything—while often losing access to land previously used for agriculture, grazing, or resource collection.

This structure creates a financial incentive to maximize the baseline threat level: the higher the projected deforestation, the more credits can be issued. The certification bodies auditing these baselines are funded by fees from the developers whose claims they're evaluating—a conflict of interest that parallels the role of credit rating agencies in the 2008 financial crisis, where agencies were paid by the banks whose securities they rated (Fern, 2023; Greenpeace International, 2023).

"165 million credits annually. Not because African forests were more threatened—the continent's combination of forest cover and weaker formal land tenure systems made projects cheaper to establish and certify."

Part II

The Fraud — Phantom Credits and Inflated Baselines

In January 2023, The Guardian and Carbon Plan published an investigation that broke the voluntary carbon market's core claim: that the credits were real.

Kariba REDD+ covered 785,000 hectares of Zimbabwean woodland. For two decades, it sold credits to Shell, Volkswagen, Delta Airlines. Verra-certified. Auditors reviewed them repeatedly. By every formal measure, legitimate. Carbon Plan's analysis found that Kariba had set its deforestation baseline at four times the actual rate in comparable forests (West et al., Carbon Plan, January 2023). Up to 94% of the 2.4 million credits issued represented emissions reductions that never happened. These findings were subsequently corroborated in peer-reviewed research published in Science (Badgley et al., 2024), which found systematic overestimation of baseline deforestation rates across forest carbon offset projects globally, with African projects showing the highest inflation. Corporations paid millions for nothing.

But Kariba wasn't rogue. An Earthsight investigation the following year found the same pattern across multiple African projects (Earthsight, 2024). The problem wasn't one bad developer. It was a certification system designed to let this happen.

The Liberia Deal

In late 2022, Liberia's government signed away rights to one million hectares—10% of the country's land—to Blue Carbon LLC, a Dubai-registered company (Vice, 2023; Financial Times, 2023). No public disclosure. No parliamentary approval. No consultation with the communities living there. The contracts gave Blue Carbon control over land use, logging rights, and carbon revenue. Communities had no legal standing to object because their customary rights over the forest had never been formally recognized—a direct artifact of colonial land law that converted customary lands to "public lands" under state control (CorpWatch, 2023). Liberia's land framework still classifies these forests as state land, established during the country's founding and treating customary tenure as informal (Laltaika & Askew, 2018).

Forty African civil society organizations called it what it was: "a new scramble for Africa's forests" (ROAPE, 2024). The Pan-African Climate Justice Alliance, representing over 1,000 organizations across 48 African countries, issued a statement calling for a moratorium on new carbon projects until community land rights protections are strengthened (PACJA, 2023).

The technical mechanism: how baselines produce phantom credits

To issue a carbon credit, a project must demonstrate it prevented emissions that "would have occurred" without it. This requires a baseline—a model of what the forest would look like if the project didn't exist. The baseline is not a measurement. It's a prediction about a counterfactual world.

Verra's methodology allows developers to draw baselines from a "reference region"—nearby forests supposedly similar to the project area. If those reference forests have high deforestation rates, the project can claim large emissions reductions even if the project forest wouldn't have been meaningfully deforested.

For Kariba, Carbon Plan found the reference regions included forests with deforestation rates far higher than the project area had historically experienced (West et al., 2023). The result: a baseline overstating the emissions threat by roughly 400%.

The incentive is structural. The more threatened you make your forest appear, the more credits you sell. The developer wants a high baseline. The certifier is paid by the developer. The auditor is paid by the developer. The community has no seat at the table.

What happened to communities in the projects

In Uganda, the Oakland Institute documented communities in Kiboga and Mubende evicted from land they'd farmed for decades to make way for New Forests Company plantations generating carbon and timber credits (Oakland Institute, 2023). Compensation was negligible. Legal processes were absent.

In Madagascar, communities relying on forest-edge agriculture inside Makira Natural Park faced intensified enforcement since the carbon project began (New Lines Magazine, 2024). Park rangers, partially funded by credit revenues, destroyed crops and confiscated land. Revenues from $18 million in credit sales flowed to the Wildlife Conservation Society and the Malagasy government. Affected households received less than 3%.

In Tanzania's Kilosa District, a peer-reviewed study compared enrolled and non-enrolled villages. Enrolled households showed a statistically significant 14% decline in caloric sufficiency compared to control villages (Asiyanbi & Lund, World Development, 2023). The payments averaged under $10 per household per year—far below the value of the agricultural expansion they were prevented from pursuing.

This last finding is causal evidence, not anecdote. The carbon market didn't deliver development alongside conservation. It substituted a tiny cash transfer for foregone agricultural growth, and the substitution made communities measurably worse off.

Part IV

The Legal Infrastructure — How Colonial Land Law Makes This Possible

The cases above aren't the product of bad actors exploiting an otherwise sound system. They're what the system reliably produces when it operates on top of colonial land tenure infrastructure.

Colonial land alienation rested on interlocking assumptions: African tenure systems were informal and therefore not "real" property. African communities were failing to use land "productively." External actors had the right and duty to improve that land's use. Under these premises, colonial administrations transferred vast tracts to European settlers and companies. Communities whose livelihoods depended on that land had no standing in the courts governing the transfers.

The mechanism was the doctrine of "waste lands" and "crown lands"—uncultivated or communally managed land was classified as belonging to the state. Post-independence governments largely inherited these classifications (Olanya, ECAS4). A government-to-company agreement like the Liberia Blue Carbon deal doesn't require consulting communities, because the forest is legally the government's to lease. That legal reality traces directly to colonial land legislation.

The UN Food and Agriculture Organization's 2020 assessment found that less than 10% of rural land in Sub-Saharan Africa is formally documented, with customary systems governing the majority of productive land but lacking legal recognition in carbon credit frameworks (FAO, 2020). Liberia's Public Lands Law still in effect derives from the 1956 framework; Kenya's Forest Act 2016 improved but didn't fully resolve customary rights recognition (Katila et al., World Development, 2019).

The mechanism is different. The moral framing is different. But the structure is similar: distant powers are again financializing African land for their own benefit, while the communities on that land absorb the cost and have limited legal standing to refuse it (Olanya, ECAS4; Laltaika & Askew, 2018; ROAPE, 2024).

The consent problem: how FPIC fails in practice

Verra and Gold Standard both require Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC) from communities before certification. FPIC is derived from UN declarations on Indigenous peoples' rights. On paper, it's a meaningful standard. In practice, it's been documented as deeply inadequate.

In Kajiado County, Kenya, a 2024 Greenpeace Africa investigation found consent obtained through meetings that excluded women and young herders—the people most dependent on restricted grazing land (Greenpeace Africa, 2024). Contracts were signed by male elders whose authority under Maasai customary law doesn't extend to alienating grazing rights. Yet the project was certified as community-consented.

Similar documentation exists for Uganda's New Forests evictions (Olanya, ECAS4) and DRC's Mai Ndombe consultation process (Fern, 2023).

The structural issue: FPIC audits are conducted by auditors paid by developers. There's no independent verification of whether consent was meaningful. Legal scholars studying FPIC have documented a pattern of "procedural compliance without substantive consent," where formal consultation occurs but communities lack meaningful veto power or access to independent legal counsel (Schilling-Vacaflor & Lenschow, Global Environmental Politics, 2022).

Part VI

Progress on Climate, Regression on Everything Else

Carbon credits were supposed to correct a market failure: rich countries externalizing climate costs onto everyone else. Instead, they built a new one. The communities whose forests generate the credits bear the costs of conservation. The corporations whose emissions created the demand capture the benefits. The mechanism looks like a market. The structure is a transfer.

The Tanzania Kilosa study is the clearest evidence (Asiyanbi & Lund, 2023). Enrolled and non-enrolled villages in the same district, comparable on observable characteristics—enrollment in REDD+ was associated with measurable decline in household welfare. The carbon payments did not compensate for foregone agricultural growth. This is not anecdote. It is causal evidence that a climate mechanism designed to produce development co-benefits produced the opposite.

Who gains

Corporations purchase the right to continue emitting while claiming neutrality. Project developers capture 55–65% of credit revenue (Oakland Institute, 2023). Global carbon accounting gains offsets that delay structural decarbonization. Wealthy-country governments gain flexibility in Paris Agreement commitments. World Bank analysis found that less than 15% of project revenues reached local communities, with the majority captured by intermediaries and certification costs (World Bank, 2023).

The real bottleneck: property rights

Carbon markets require legal clarity about who controls sequestration rights. Without formally recognized community tenure, that clarity defaults to whoever holds state title—typically the government or a developer. Carbon markets formalized one layer of the asset (sequestration rights) for investors while leaving the underlying land rights of communities informal and therefore vulnerable. This inverts the logic development economists identify: the market created capital from the forest without creating rights for the people in it. The result is not a market. It is a transfer (Benjaminsen & Bryceson, 2023).

The ACMI problem: scaling dispossession at COP27

The Africa Carbon Markets Initiative, launched at COP27 in Egypt in 2022, was presented as African ownership of carbon finance. It was endorsed by African finance ministers and positioned as a mechanism for African governments to direct climate revenue toward domestic development. The initiative targets 300 million credits annually by 2030 but does not specify mandatory community tenure recognition as a prerequisite for certification (ACMI, Roadmap Report, 2022).

ACMI operates within the same VCM architecture that has produced the outcomes documented above. African governments may receive higher revenue shares. But revenue flowing to governments does not flow to communities. The Liberia Blue Carbon deal was signed by the Liberian government—a government-level transaction that left communities legally excluded.

A legitimate alternative requires: mandatory community land titling before any project can be certified; revenue-sharing minimums enforced by statute; certification bodies funded publicly or by buyers—not developers; and meaningful legal standing for communities to challenge registrations covering their land.

The Verdict

The carbon credit, as it currently operates in much of Africa, is a certificate of emissions reduction for the buyer and a certificate of land alienation for the community. Progress on climate. Regression on everything else. That is not a win-win. It is the same arithmetic that governed the colonial resource economy: the gains go to whoever controls the legal architecture, and the costs go to whoever does not.

The data shows a transfer: developers capture 55–65% of credit revenue (Oakland Institute, 2023), certification fees take another 20–30%, and communities receive single-digit percentages while losing access to land (Fern, 2023). The Tanzania study found enrolled communities measurably worse off in caloric sufficiency than control villages (Asiyanbi & Lund, 2023). The pattern is consistent across projects because the legal architecture—unrecognized customary tenure meeting state-to-developer agreements—produces it reliably.
