Who Pays When Corporations Buy Carbon Credits from African Forests?

Research Question

When a corporation buys a carbon credit from an African forest, who actually pays the price?

By Sara

In 2023, Shell Oil reported that it had purchased carbon credits from Kariba REDD+, one of Africa's largest forest offset projects, as part of its climate neutrality strategy. Kariba covers 785,000 hectares of Zimbabwean miombo woodland. For two decades, it sold credits to Shell, Volkswagen, and Delta Airlines. Communities living in and around the project area lost access to forests they had used for generations to gather food, fuel, and materials. They received almost nothing in return.

When investigators analyzed Kariba's methodology, they found that up to 94% of its credits were phantom. The project had inflated deforestation projections by roughly 400% (West et al., 2023; Badgley et al., 2024). The corporations that bought them paid millions for emissions reductions that never happened. Shell got a climate-neutral claim. The communities got dispossessed. This is not an exception. This is how the system works.

The voluntary carbon market is the private, unregulated system where corporations buy forest offsets to meet net-zero pledges. It was designed to solve a problem: wealthy countries externalizing climate costs onto everyone else. The market would channel capital to climate-vulnerable regions, funding both conservation and local development. Two decades in, that promise looks more like extraction.

Sub-Saharan Africa now supplies more forest carbon credits than any other region. Across ten countries, a consistent pattern has emerged: contracts signed by governments without community consultation, certified by auditors paid by developers, justified by the language of climate action. Communities lose access to land. Developers capture 55 to 65% of credit revenue (Oakland Institute, 2023). Certification fees take another 20 to 30% (Fern, 2023). What reaches communities often amounts to less than 5% (World Bank, 2023). Sometimes nothing at all.

The legal mechanism enabling this is not new. It is the same infrastructure that governed colonial land alienation: state control over lands classified as "public" or "unimproved," the legal invisibility of customary tenure, external certification of contracts that bypass those who live on the land. The justification has shifted from civilization and development to sustainability and conservation. The structure endures.

African civil society organizations and scholars have a term for this: green colonialism. Not as metaphor, but as description of a legal and financial system reactivating colonial land law to financialize African forests (Olanya, ECAS4; Laltaika & Askew, 2018; ROAPE, 2024). The carbon registries certifying these projects are playing the role that colonial land courts once did. They legitimize transfers that disconnect people from land that sustains them, with no legal standing to refuse.

This project argues three things together. First, phantom credits are not the work of rogue developers. They follow from certification incentives: auditors paid by developers, baselines set by developers, communities excluded from the process. Second, the land rights precarity that makes dispossession possible is not accidental. It is inherited colonial land law, still operating under post-independence statutes. Third, the money confirms the transfer: developers and intermediaries capture 75 to 90% of credit value while communities lose access to land and receive single-digit shares, if anything. Connecting fraud, tenure, and revenue in one frame is what this project adds.

A carbon credit is a certificate for one metric ton of CO₂ either kept out of the atmosphere or removed from it. Corporations buy them to count toward net-zero pledges without cutting their own emissions. Forest projects dominate because trees store carbon: if a forest stays standing, that storage counts as an avoided emission. That is the logic. The question is who pays when the certificate gets sold.

Part I

The Promise: Markets Would Save the Planet and Fund Development

Carbon credits are financial instruments designed to put a price on emissions reductions. One credit represents one metric ton of CO₂ either prevented from entering the atmosphere or removed from it. If a company in Germany can't cheaply reduce its own emissions, it can pay for reductions elsewhere, like protecting a forest in Zimbabwe that would otherwise be cleared, and count those reductions against its own climate targets.

This mechanism was formalized in 1997 under the Kyoto Protocol's Clean Development Mechanism (CDM), the first international carbon credit system. Rich countries would fund emissions cuts in poor countries at a fraction of domestic cost. Host countries would receive investment and technology transfer. The planet would get lower emissions overall. Climate action and development, together.

The Voluntary Carbon Market emerged in the 2000s as the private, unregulated version of this idea. Companies bought credits voluntarily to meet their own net-zero pledges, driven by shareholder pressure, ESG ratings, and consumer expectations. By 2021, the market was worth over $2 billion annually, with projections hitting $50 billion by 2030 (Ecosystem Marketplace, 2021). Within this market, REDD+ (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation) became the dominant project type, essentially payments to not cut down forests. Most credits are certified by Verra under its Verified Carbon Standard (VCS), the dominant private registry, or by Gold Standard.

Africa became the primary supplier. The continent holds approximately 25% of the world's tropical forests (FAO, 2020), vast savanna ecosystems, and, critically for developers, weaker formal land tenure systems. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization found that less than 10% of rural land in Sub-Saharan Africa is formally documented, with customary systems governing most productive land but lacking legal recognition in international frameworks (FAO, 2020). In carbon market terms, this translated to cheap, fast-to-establish supply. By 2023, Africa was issuing 165 million voluntary carbon credits annually, more than any other region (Ecosystem Marketplace, 2023). Investigations found that African projects showed higher rates of community displacement and lower revenue-sharing ratios than comparable projects in Latin America, attributed primarily to differences in land tenure documentation and enforcement capacity (Forest Peoples Programme, 2023).

How carbon credits are generated and sold

Step 1: A developer identifies a forested area and negotiates rights to the carbon stored in it, through direct purchase, long-term lease, or government agreement.

Step 2: The developer establishes a "baseline," a projection of how much deforestation would occur without the project. This becomes the counterfactual against which avoided emissions are measured. It is not a measurement; it is a prediction about a world that didn't happen.

Step 3: Third-party auditors verify the baseline and project design according to standards set by Verra or Gold Standard. The auditors are paid by the developer.

Step 4: Credits are issued, one per metric ton of CO₂ claimed as avoided emissions, then registered and sold on voluntary markets.

Step 5: Corporations purchase credits to offset their own emissions, claiming carbon neutrality in sustainability reports and marketing.

Step 6: Revenue flows primarily to the developer and certification intermediaries. Communities typically receive a small fraction (if anything), while often losing access to land previously used for agriculture, grazing, or resource collection.

This structure creates a financial incentive to maximize the baseline threat level: the higher the projected deforestation, the more credits can be issued. The certification bodies auditing these baselines are funded by fees from the developers whose claims they're evaluating, a conflict of interest that parallels the role of credit rating agencies in the 2008 financial crisis, where agencies were paid by the banks whose securities they rated (Fern, 2023; Greenpeace International, 2023).

Part II

The First Scramble: Colonial Land Alienation and Its Legacy

Between 1880 and 1914, European control of Africa expanded from 10% to 90% of the continent, an annexation of unprecedented speed and scale (Pakenham, 1991). The most intense period, 1884–1905, saw the legal and administrative transformation of African land tenure systems across nearly an entire continent in just two decades.

The legal mechanism was remarkably consistent across British, French, German, Belgian, and Portuguese territories. The Berlin Conference's doctrine of "effective occupation" (General Act, Article 35) required colonial powers to demonstrate administrative presence to claim territory, a standard that privileged European state structures and dismissed existing African governance systems (Craven, 2015). Colonial administrations then classified African communal lands as "waste lands," "crown lands," or terra nullius (land belonging to no one). The logic was straightforward: if land wasn't privately owned and enclosed in the European style, it was available for the state to allocate.

Colonial land law systematically reclassified customary tenure as mere "usufruct" (use rights), while vesting ownership in the colonial state, a legal transformation that made African communities tenants on their own land (Chanock, 1991). Kenya's Crown Lands Ordinance of 1915 classified all untitled land as state property, enabling the colonial administration to allocate approximately 20% of the colony's most fertile land to European settlers (Okoth-Ogendo, 1991). The pattern repeated across the continent.

This wasn't incidental. It was the foundation of the colonial economy. In the Congo Free State, Leopold II granted rubber concessions covering approximately 1.5 million square kilometers to private companies (an area larger than Western Europe), with no legal obligation to the Congolese communities whose land and labor sustained the extraction (Hochschild, 1998). These concessions operated through the same legal structure repeated everywhere: the state owned the land, private companies leased extraction rights, and communities had no legal standing to object.

The infrastructure built during this period reveals the underlying logic. Jedwab and Moradi's (2016) analysis of African railway construction found that 90% of colonial-era lines ran from extraction sites to ports rather than connecting population centers, a pattern associated with 15–20% lower long-term GDP and urbanization outcomes compared to countries with more internally-connected rail networks. Railways ran from mines to ports, not between African towns. Wealth flowed to Europe; the costs (displacement, forced labor, environmental degradation, and the dismantling of existing economic systems) remained in Africa.

When African nations gained independence in the 1950s–70s, they inherited this legal architecture largely intact. Kenya's land titling system still operates under legislation derived from the 1959 colonial ordinance; Tanzania's 1999 Land Acts preserve the colonial distinction between "native reserves" and state land (Manji, 2006). Liberia's Public Lands Law derives from its 1956 codification and has never been substantially reformed. The result: World Bank analysis found that only 10% of rural land in Sub-Saharan Africa is formally documented, with 90% held under customary systems that lack legal recognition in national registries, a direct legacy of colonial land law that classified customary tenure as informal "use rights" rather than ownership (Byamugisha, 2013).

Development economists have documented how this matters. De Soto (2000) estimated that informal property rights trap trillions in "dead capital," assets that can't function in formal capital markets because they lack legal documentation. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson's (2001) cross-country analysis found that former colonies designed for resource extraction rather than settlement developed institutions associated with 70% lower GDP per capita over the long term. The infrastructure deficit, educational gaps, and institutional fragility that shape African development today trace directly to the extractive model established during the Scramble.

This history isn't distant. It is the foundation on which carbon markets are now operating. The same tenure precarity that enabled colonial concessions enables carbon concessions. The same state control over "public" land allows governments to sign away forest rights without community consultation. The same legal invisibility of customary rights means communities have no standing to challenge certifications. The language has shifted from civilization and progress to sustainability and climate action. The structure endures.

African civil society organizations and researchers have explicitly termed the expansion of carbon markets a "new scramble." The Oakland Institute's 2023 report titled the phenomenon "Green Colonialism 2.0," documenting how carbon projects repeat historical patterns of land alienation under the banner of climate action (Oakland Institute, 2023). The parallel is not rhetorical. It is structural and legal.

Part III

The Fraud: Phantom Credits and Inflated Baselines

In January 2023, The Guardian and Carbon Plan published an investigation that broke the voluntary carbon market's core claim: that the credits were real.

Kariba REDD+ covered 785,000 hectares of Zimbabwean woodland. For two decades, it sold credits to Shell, Volkswagen, Delta Airlines, all Verra-certified, repeatedly audited, and formally legitimate. Carbon Plan's analysis found that Kariba had set its deforestation baseline at four times the actual rate in comparable forests (West et al., 2023). Up to 94% of the 2.4 million credits issued represented emissions reductions that never happened. These findings were subsequently corroborated in peer-reviewed research published in Science (Badgley et al., 2024), which found systematic baseline overestimation across forest carbon offset projects globally, with African projects showing the highest inflation rates. Corporations had paid millions for nothing.

Kariba wasn't rogue. An Earthsight investigation the following year found the same pattern across multiple African projects (Earthsight, 2024). The problem wasn't one bad developer. It was a certification system with structural incentives to let this happen.

The Liberia Deal

In late 2022, Liberia's government signed away rights to one million hectares (10% of the country's land) to Blue Carbon LLC, a Dubai-registered company (Vice, 2023; Financial Times, 2023). No public disclosure. No parliamentary approval. No consultation with the communities living there. The contracts gave Blue Carbon control over land use, logging rights, and carbon revenue. Communities had no legal standing to object because their customary rights had never been formally recognized, a direct artifact of colonial land law that converted customary lands to "public lands" under state control (CorpWatch, 2023; Laltaika & Askew, 2018).

Forty African civil society organizations called it what it was: "a new scramble for Africa's forests" (ROAPE, 2024). The Pan-African Climate Justice Alliance, representing over 1,000 organizations across 48 African countries, issued a statement calling for a moratorium on new carbon projects until community land rights protections are strengthened (PACJA, 2023).

The technical mechanism: how baselines produce phantom credits

To issue a carbon credit, a project must demonstrate it prevented emissions that "would have occurred" without it. This requires a baseline, a model of what the forest would look like if the project didn't exist. The baseline is not a measurement. It's a prediction about a counterfactual world.

Verra's methodology allows developers to draw baselines from a "reference region," nearby forests supposedly similar to the project area. If those reference forests have high deforestation rates, the project can claim large emissions reductions even if the project forest would never have been meaningfully deforested.

For Kariba, Carbon Plan found that reference regions included forests with deforestation rates far higher than the project area had historically experienced (West et al., 2023). The result was a baseline overstating the emissions threat by roughly 400%.

The incentive is structural. The more threatened you make your forest appear, the more credits you sell. The developer wants a high baseline. The certifier is paid by the developer. The auditor is paid by the developer. The community has no seat at the table. No one in this chain has a financial interest in an accurate, conservative baseline.

What happened to communities in these projects

In Uganda, the Oakland Institute documented communities in Kiboga and Mubende evicted from land they'd farmed for decades to make way for New Forests Company plantations generating carbon and timber credits (Oakland Institute, 2023). Compensation was negligible. Legal processes were absent. Families who returned found their crops destroyed.

In Madagascar, communities relying on forest-edge agriculture inside Makira Natural Park faced intensified enforcement once the carbon project began (New Lines Magazine, 2024). Park rangers, partially funded by credit revenues, destroyed crops and confiscated land. Of $18 million in total credit sales, revenues flowed primarily to the Wildlife Conservation Society and the Malagasy government. Affected households received less than 3%.

In Tanzania's Kilosa District, a peer-reviewed study compared enrolled and non-enrolled villages in the same district. Enrolled households showed a statistically significant 14% decline in caloric sufficiency compared to control villages (Asiyanbi & Lund, World Development, 2023). Carbon payments averaged under $10 per household per year, far below the value of the agricultural expansion they were prevented from pursuing. The carbon market didn't deliver development alongside conservation. It substituted a tiny cash transfer for foregone agricultural growth, and the substitution made communities measurably worse off.

Part V

The Legal Infrastructure: How Colonial Land Law Makes This Possible

The legal invisibility of customary tenure is the single mechanism connecting 1884 to today. Colonial administrations classified communal land as state property because communities held it without European-style title. Post-independence governments kept those classifications. Carbon developers operate on the same premise: if customary rights aren't in the registry, the state can lease the forest.

One mechanism. 140 years. Still running.

These cases aren't the product of bad actors exploiting an otherwise sound system. They're what the system reliably produces when it operates on top of colonial land tenure infrastructure.

Colonial land alienation rested on interlocking assumptions: African tenure systems were informal and therefore not "real" property. African communities were failing to use land "productively." External actors had the right and duty to improve that land's use. Under these premises, colonial administrations transferred vast tracts to European settlers and companies. Communities whose livelihoods depended on that land had no standing in the courts governing the transfers.

The specific mechanism was the doctrine of "waste lands" and "crown lands": uncultivated or communally managed land was classified as belonging to the state. Post-independence governments largely inherited these classifications (Olanya, ECAS4). A government-to-company agreement like the Liberia Blue Carbon deal doesn't require consulting communities because the forest is legally the government's to lease. That reality traces directly to colonial land legislation. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization found less than 10% of rural land in Sub-Saharan Africa is formally documented, with customary systems governing most productive land but lacking recognition in carbon credit frameworks (FAO, 2020). Liberia's Public Lands Law still in effect derives from the 1956 framework; Kenya's Forest Act 2016 improved but didn't fully resolve customary rights recognition (Katila et al., 2019).

The mechanism is different. The moral framing is different. The structure is similar: distant powers are again financializing African land for their own benefit, while the communities on that land absorb the cost and have limited legal standing to refuse it (Olanya, ECAS4; Laltaika & Askew, 2018; ROAPE, 2024).

The consent problem: how FPIC fails in practice

Verra and Gold Standard both require Free, Prior, and Informed Consent from communities before certification. FPIC is derived from UN declarations on Indigenous peoples' rights. On paper, it's a meaningful standard. In practice, documentation of its failure is consistent across projects.

In Kajiado County, Kenya, a 2024 Greenpeace Africa investigation found consent obtained through meetings that excluded women and young herders, the people most dependent on restricted grazing land. Contracts were signed by male elders whose authority under Maasai customary law doesn't extend to unilaterally alienating grazing rights from the community. The project was certified as community-consented (Greenpeace Africa, 2024).

Similar documentation exists for Uganda's New Forests Company evictions (Olanya, ECAS4) and DRC's Mai Ndombe REDD+ consultation process (Fern, 2023). The structural issue is the same across all of them: FPIC audits are conducted by auditors paid by developers, with no independent verification of whether consent was substantive. Legal scholars studying FPIC have documented a pattern of "procedural compliance without substantive consent": formal consultation occurs, but communities lack meaningful veto power or access to independent legal counsel (Schilling-Vacaflor & Lenschow, 2022).

"Community land rights are routinely subordinated to the commercial interests of carbon project developers. The legal mechanisms enabling this dispossession are not new; they are the same colonial-era provisions that classified customary lands as state property available for external appropriation."

Part VII

Progress on Climate, Regression on Everything Else

Carbon credits were supposed to correct a market failure: rich countries externalizing climate costs onto everyone else. Instead, they built a new one. The communities whose forests generate the credits bear the costs of conservation. The corporations whose emissions created the demand capture the benefits. The mechanism looks like a market; the structure is a transfer.

The Tanzania Kilosa study is the clearest evidence (Asiyanbi & Lund, 2023). Using a quasi-experimental design (enrolled and non-enrolled villages in the same district, comparable on observable characteristics), enrollment in REDD+ was associated with measurable decline in household welfare. Carbon payments did not compensate for foregone agricultural growth. This is not anecdote. It is causal evidence that a climate mechanism designed to produce development co-benefits produced the opposite.

Who gains

Corporations purchase the right to continue emitting while claiming neutrality. Project developers capture 55–65% of credit revenue (Oakland Institute, 2023). Global carbon accounting gains offsets that delay structural decarbonization. Wealthy-country governments gain flexibility in Paris Agreement commitments. World Bank analysis found less than 15% of project revenues reached local communities, with the majority captured by intermediaries and certification costs (World Bank, 2023).

The real bottleneck: property rights

Carbon markets require legal clarity about who controls sequestration rights. Without formally recognized community tenure, that clarity defaults to whoever holds state title: the government or a developer. Carbon markets have inverted the development logic: they formalized the asset (sequestration rights) for investors while leaving the underlying land rights of communities informal and therefore vulnerable. The market created capital from the forest without creating rights for the people in it (Benjaminsen & Bryceson, 2023).

The ACMI problem: scaling dispossession at COP27

The Africa Carbon Markets Initiative, launched at COP27 in Egypt in 2022, was presented as African ownership of carbon finance, endorsed by African finance ministers, targeted at 300 million credits annually by 2030. But ACMI operates within the same VCM architecture that produced the outcomes documented above. Its founding documents do not specify mandatory community tenure recognition as a prerequisite for certification (ACMI, Roadmap Report, 2022).

African governments may receive higher revenue shares under ACMI. But revenue flowing to governments does not flow to communities. The Liberia Blue Carbon deal was signed by the Liberian government, a government-level transaction that left communities legally excluded. Scaling ACMI within this framework means scaling the problem, not solving it.

A legitimate alternative requires mandatory community land titling before any project can be certified, revenue-sharing minimums enforced by statute rather than developer choice, certification bodies funded publicly rather than by the developers being certified, and meaningful legal standing for communities to challenge registrations covering their land.

The Verdict

The carbon credit, as it currently operates in much of Africa, is a certificate of emissions reduction for the buyer and a certificate of land alienation for the community. The data shows a transfer: developers capture 55–65% of credit revenue (Oakland Institute, 2023), certification fees take another 20–30%, and communities receive single-digit percentages while losing access to land (Fern, 2023). The Tanzania study found enrolled communities measurably worse off in caloric sufficiency than control villages (Asiyanbi & Lund, 2023). The pattern is consistent because the legal architecture of unrecognized customary tenure meeting state-to-developer agreements reliably produces it.

This doesn't mean carbon markets are irredeemable. It means a carbon market built on unrecognized land rights will reproduce dispossession. It means the certification architecture, funded by developers and audited by auditors hired by developers, cannot catch the fraud it has structural incentives to overlook. Progress on climate. Regression on everything else. That is not a win-win.

What would reform look like?

Reform Impact Score: 0/4
Communities with formal tenure have legal standing to refuse or negotiate project terms. Without it, a government-to-developer agreement requires no community sign-off. Mandatory titling before certification would end the legal invisibility that makes dispossession structurally possible.
Voluntary benefit-sharing frameworks have produced community shares as low as 0–3%. A statutory minimum, say, 50% of net revenue, would fundamentally restructure who carbon markets work for. Developer profit margins would compress. Projects with genuinely low community benefit would become unviable.
Verra and Gold Standard are funded by fees from the developers whose projects they certify. This is the same conflict of interest that characterized credit rating agencies before 2008. Public or independent funding would remove the structural incentive to approve inflated baselines and inadequate consent processes.
Currently, communities have no formal right to challenge a Verra certification covering their land. Legal standing, the right to appear before a certifying body and contest a registration, would give communities a veto that FPIC on paper currently does not. It would also create a paper trail of contested certifications that markets could not ignore.

This is what a carbon market that doesn't reproduce colonial dispossession would require. None of these are currently mandatory under Verra or Gold Standard.

What this project contributes

This site builds three original syntheses from secondary sources. The revenue flow diagram in Part VI combines Oakland Institute, Fern, and World Bank estimates into a single $15 credit breakdown. The colonial vs. carbon market comparison table in Part V maps the same legal mechanisms across two eras side by side. The Africa credits growth chart annotates registry data with the policy events that accelerated issuance after 2020. None of these datasets existed in one place before this project assembled them.

The limits are real. Revenue-sharing figures come from investigative reporting and NGO research, not audited developer disclosures. Project developers dispute some community-share estimates, and Wildlife Works has contested the West et al. baseline analysis. I cite the peer-reviewed version of each disputed claim where one exists. Specific dollar figures should be read as estimates. The structural argument, that unrecognized customary tenure enables dispossession, is supported across sources that developers do not dispute.
